The core of this endeavor lies in Electroencephalography (EEG), a non-invasive technique that measures voltage fluctuations resulting from ionic current flows within the neurons of the brain . As one of the standard methods for measuring brain activity and the primary source of signals for noninvasive Brain-Computer Interfaces (BCIs), EEG provides the necessary raw material for any attempt at neural-to-musical translation . However, the path from raw EEG data to a coherent musical note is fraught with technical challenges and requires a deliberate framework for interpretation. The history of converting brain signals into sound begins not in a laboratory but in the realm of experimental music. The seminal work in this area is Alvin Lucier's Music for Solo Performer (1965), often referred to as the 'brain wave piece' . In this composition, Lucier attached an EEG electrode to his scalp, which was connected to a drum set. His alpha brainwaves, when relaxed, would trigger a transistor amplifier to activate the drumsticks, causing them to strike the instrument . This piece is a landmark because it uses the brain as a reactive performance instrument; the music is a direct, albeit indirect, consequence of the performer's state of consciousness. It is not a composition written by the brain, but a sound generated by it. This work established the foundational idea of using neurophysiological signals as a source for art, but its methodology is fundamentally different from creating a structured musical track.
