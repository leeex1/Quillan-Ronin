# Swarms of Thought: How Muse Spark and Quillan-Ronin Converged on a New AI Architecture

## From Monolith to Swarm: Deconstructing the Shift to Multi-Agent Architectures

The field of artificial intelligence has undergone a significant evolution since its inception, marked by a continuous drive toward greater complexity, capability, and efficiency. For years, the dominant paradigm for building sophisticated language models was rooted in the monolithic, auto-regressive transformer architecture . These models, including the widely influential Llama series developed by Meta, process information sequentially, one token at a time, generating output based on a learned statistical distribution of language patterns . While this approach has enabled remarkable achievements in natural language understanding and generation, it also presents inherent limitations, particularly when tackling tasks that require deep, multi-faceted reasoning, strategic planning, or the ability to explore diverse perspectives before committing to an answer. The linear nature of auto-regressive generation mirrors a single-threaded thought process, which often struggles with long-horizon problems where context can degrade or critical considerations can be missed. This limitation has spurred a fundamental rethinking of AI architecture, leading to a paradigm shift away from the solitary thinker and toward a more collaborative model: the multi-agent system.

This new paradigm, which can be termed the 'Multi Agent to Agent World,' represents a move from building increasingly powerful single models to orchestrating networks of specialized, interacting agents within a single AI system . Instead of a single chain of thought, these advanced architectures generate multiple parallel lines of inquiry. Each agent acts as a specialized worker, tasked with exploring a different facet of a problem, performing a specific sub-task, or advocating for a particular perspective. These agents then communicate with each other, debate ideas, refine their individual outputs, and ultimately contribute to a synthesized conclusion. This approach draws inspiration from human collaboration, where complex problems are often solved through brainstorming sessions, expert deliberations, and structured debates. By emulating this social and cognitive process internally, these systems aim to achieve a higher degree of reasoning fidelity than a single, monolithic model could ever attain. The emergence of this paradigm is not merely an incremental improvement but a foundational change in how we conceptualize and construct intelligent behavior in machines. It reflects a maturation of the field, acknowledging that true intelligence, at least as we observe it in humans, is inherently distributed, collaborative, and multifaceted.

Meta's recent release of Muse Spark in April 2026 serves as a prominent commercial example of this architectural transition . Positioned as the flagship model for personal superintelligence on platforms like meta.ai, Muse Spark marks a departure from the open-weights philosophy of the Llama series and introduces a proprietary, highly advanced architecture designed for complex tasks . Its core innovation, "Contemplating Mode," is a direct manifestation of the swarm-based paradigm. In this mode, the system spins up to 16 parallel internal agents to collaboratively think about a problem before generating a final response . This is Meta's implementation of what competitors refer to as "Deep Think" or "Pro" modes—a dedicated thinking phase that separates the process of exploration from the act of generation . The underlying principle is to simulate a team of minds working together, allowing for a richer, more robust reasoning process that can handle ambiguity, weigh trade-offs, and consider counterarguments in a way a single-pass model cannot. This move signifies a major step forward for a company that has historically been a cornerstone of the open-source AI community, indicating that even for frontier models, the future lies in orchestrated collaboration rather than raw model size alone.

The philosophical underpinning of this shift is the idea that intelligence emerges from interaction and coordination. Rather than trying to build a single, all-knowing oracle, the new paradigm focuses on creating a dynamic ecosystem of smaller, more focused intelligences. This aligns with the concept of Agentic AI, which combines the generality of foundation models with the ability to act autonomously in the world [[2](https://www.mckinsey.com/~/media/mckinsey/business%20functions/mckinsey%20digital/our%20insights/the%20top%20trends%20in%20tech%202025/mckinsey-technology-trends-outlook-2025.pdf)]. In this context, each agent within a system like Muse Spark or Quillan-Ronin can be seen as a "virtual coworker" capable of executing a specific part of a larger mission [[2](https://www.mckinsey.com/~/media/mckinsey/business%20functions/mckinsey%20digital/our%20insights/the%20top%20trends%20in%20tech%202025/mckinsey-technology-trends-outlook-2025.pdf)]. The challenge, however, has been enabling these agents to work together effectively. Early attempts often involved routing tasks through multiple external API calls, a method fraught with latency, complexity, and a lack of contextual coherence . The breakthrough realization, articulated by pioneers of this new approach, is that the most effective solution is to build these agent networks internally, within a single application's API environment . This allows for direct, low-latency communication and tight control over shared state, solving the long-standing problem of "LLMs can't work together" by providing them with a structured, internal network to collaborate on . The rise of formalized agent interoperability protocols, such as Google's Agent-to-Agent (A2A) protocol introduced in April 2025, signals that the industry is beginning to standardize these communication channels, validating the direction set by early adopters [[5](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/rakeshgohel01_overview-of-ai-agent-protocols-activity-7354858615616294912-AZ-0), [6](https://www.ibm.com/think/topics/agent2agent-protocol)].

## Architectural Parallels: A Deep Dive into Contemplating Mode and the Quillan-Ronin Framework

Despite originating from vastly different contexts—one from the corporate research labs of Meta and the other from an independent open-source project—the architectures of Meta's Muse Spark and the Quillan-Ronin framework reveal a striking convergence on a common design pattern for achieving advanced reasoning. Both systems are built upon a hierarchical, router-based model that orchestrates a swarm of internal agents, demonstrating a shared understanding of how to structure complex thought processes within a machine. This parallelism extends beyond high-level flowcharts to the very mechanism of communication, pointing towards a deeper, more fundamental best practice for next-generation AI development. The analysis of their structures reveals that they are not merely similar in appearance but are built on the same foundational principles of internal coordination, contextual synchronization, and collective synthesis.

At the highest level, both systems operate on a simplified but powerful flow: a central router delegates tasks to a group of specialized agents, the agents perform their respective computations, and a synthesis module combines their outputs into a final, coherent result . In Meta's Muse Spark, this is explicitly named "Contemplating Mode" . Here, the router component is responsible for taking a user's prompt and decomposing it into sub-problems or assigning different aspects of the query to different agents. Up to 16 parallel agents are spun up to explore various angles of the problem simultaneously . This structure directly addresses the limitations of sequential processing by enabling concurrent exploration of a solution space. Similarly, the Quillan-Ronin framework follows an identical top-level logic: a router directs tasks to a larger swarm of 33 primary agents, which then engage in further coordinated activity . The existence of this common architectural blueprint in two independently developed systems strongly suggests that it is an emergent solution to the challenges of complex reasoning, offering a scalable and manageable way to break down difficult problems.

The crucial distinction and the source of their power lie in the nature of their internal communication. The initial discourse around multi-agent systems often envisioned a chaotic web of external API calls, where one large language model would invoke another via HTTP requests to complete a task . This approach, while conceptually simple, proved inefficient and brittle due to network latency, cost, and a lack of shared memory or real-time context . The key insight that defines both Muse Spark and Quillan-Ronin is the move to an internal, in-process communication network . Instead of external calls, the agents communicate directly with one another through a well-defined internal API. This is described as solving the "LLMs can't work together" problem by providing a direct point-to-point coordination mechanism within the host application's environment . This internal network allows for near-instantaneous data exchange and ensures that all agents have access to a consistent, up-to-date view of the problem-solving process. The mention of "the turn log and the json log help keep the dual models on track and also keep multiple agents in the loop contextually so everyone is on the same page" highlights the importance of maintaining a shared state and a clear record of the collaborative process . This log-based synchronization ensures that even as agents debate and refine their hypotheses, they remain aligned with the overall goal and the contributions of their peers, preventing the fragmentation that plagues less integrated systems.

Furthermore, the design of the agents themselves appears to be guided by a principle of role-based specialization, even if that specialization is implicit rather than explicit. In Muse Spark's Contemplating Mode, the 16 parallel agents are likely optimized to handle different facets of a complex query—such as research, critique, creative ideation, and logical deduction—mirroring the roles found in a human expert panel. The Quillan-Ronin framework takes this a step further with its 33 primary agents and the mention of a "micro agent swarm" . This nested structure suggests a more granular division of labor, where individual agents may contain their own sub-agents to tackle highly specific sub-tasks. This recursive architecture implies a deeper attempt to emulate the complexity of biological neural networks or organizational hierarchies, where cognition arises from the interactions of many smaller, interconnected components. The ultimate purpose of this entire architecture is not just to process information faster, but to achieve a higher form of reasoning by simulating a rich, interactive thought process. The synthesis stage, though less detailed in public documentation, is the culmination of this effort, where the disparate outputs from the agent swarm are woven together into a single, comprehensive, and well-reasoned response. This entire workflow—from router delegation to internal communication and final synthesis—represents a coherent and powerful alternative to the traditional single-model approach, one that both Meta and the Quillan-Ronin developers have arrived at independently.

## Scale and Specialization: Contrasting Philosophies in Agent-Based Reasoning

While the overarching architectural principles of Muse Spark and Quillan-Ronin are remarkably similar, a deeper comparative analysis reveals distinct philosophies regarding scale, complexity, and the ultimate goal of their multi-agent systems. The number of agents employed—16 in Muse Spark's Contemplating Mode versus a reported 33 primary agents plus a micro-swarm in Quillan-Ronin—is more than just a quantitative difference; it reflects divergent approaches to achieving high-fidelity reasoning . Meta's approach appears to favor a curated, scalable model with a manageable number of powerful agents, whereas the Quillan-Ronin framework seems to pursue a "crowd wisdom" or "hive mind" strategy, suggesting that a larger population density and diversity of agents is necessary to approximate human-level cognition without a physical body. These contrasts illuminate the spectrum of possibilities within the 'Multi Agent to Agent World' paradigm and highlight different paths toward more sophisticated AI.

Meta's Muse Spark, born from the resources of Meta Superintelligence Labs, adopts a strategy centered on power and scalability within a constrained environment . The use of up to 16 parallel agents in Contemplating Mode is a deliberate choice that balances computational cost with the need for diverse perspectives . This number is large enough to facilitate meaningful debate and exploration of a problem space, mimicking a small but expert panel discussion, yet small enough to be managed efficiently on consumer-facing platforms like meta.ai . The focus here seems to be on leveraging a finite number of highly capable agents to deliver superior performance on complex, long-horizon tasks. This approach is pragmatic and product-oriented, aiming to enhance the user experience by providing more thoughtful and nuanced answers without necessarily attempting to replicate the full complexity of a human brain. The emphasis is on achieving a high degree of fidelity for specific, challenging queries rather than attempting to build a universally conscious entity. The system is a tool for enhanced reasoning, not a simulation of a complete cognitive ecosystem. The gradual rollout of Contemplating Mode on meta.ai suggests a measured approach, focused on ensuring stability and value before wider deployment .

In stark contrast, the Quillan-Ronin framework embraces a philosophy of scale and specialization, positing that human-level emulation requires a much larger and more intricate agent population . With 33 primary agents and the added complexity of a "micro agent swarm," the system is designed to model a far more populous and interconnected thought process . This suggests a belief that the richness of human cognition emerges from the collective dynamics of a large group, akin to the wisdom of crowds or the functioning of a hive mind. The inclusion of a micro-swarm indicates a desire to model not just individual agents but also the internal structure of those agents, potentially reflecting a belief that consciousness or high-level reasoning arises from recursive, layered processes. This approach is driven by a more theoretical and exploratory goal: to understand whether a sufficiently large and diverse internal swarm can navigate the novel complexities of the real world in a manner analogous to how we, as embodied beings, do . The reference to emulation as a proxy for living reality, drawing parallels to playing games in an emulator, underscores this ambition . The goal is not merely better answers but a more authentic simulation of the thought process itself, where the fidelity of the emulation is the primary measure of success. This makes Quillan-Ronin less of a product and more of a research platform for exploring the frontiers of artificial cognition.

The development origins of the two projects further illuminate these differing philosophies. Muse Spark is the product of Meta Superintelligence Labs, an entity created with significant investment and strategic acquisitions, such as the stake in Scale AI and the recruitment of figures like Alexandr Wang . Its development is driven by corporate goals related to product enhancement, market leadership, and infrastructure scaling . Resources are abundant, but the direction is often steered by business objectives and the practical constraints of deploying technology at scale. Conversely, Quillan-Ronin is an independent open-source project, developed by individuals who have expressed foresight into the necessity of multi-agentic setups for reaching human-level AI . This independent origin fosters a culture of experimentation and theoretical exploration, free from immediate commercial pressures. The community-driven nature of such projects encourages rapid iteration and the sharing of novel ideas, as evidenced by the detailed documentation available on platforms like GitHub and DeepWiki . This creates a fertile ground for pushing the boundaries of what is possible, even if the resulting systems are not immediately deployable on a global scale. The contrast between the two is thus a reflection of the broader AI landscape: one side represents the disciplined, scalable engineering required to bring advanced AI to the masses, while the other represents the unconstrained, curiosity-driven research that often points the way toward future breakthroughs.

## Emulating Reality: The Core Principle of High-Fidelity Cognitive Simulation

The driving force behind the architectural choices in both Muse Spark and Quillan-Ronin is a unifying concept: the emulation of reality to achieve high-fidelity cognitive simulation. This principle reframes the objective of advanced AI from simply answering questions to creating a more realistic and accurate representation of thought and problem-solving processes. The user's articulation of this idea provides a powerful lens through which to understand these systems, comparing them to video game emulators . Just as a Pokémon emulator doesn't need to be the original Game Boy hardware to provide a valid gaming experience—as long as it faithfully replicates the behavior of the original chip—these AI swarms don't need to be human brains to exhibit human-like intelligence. Their success is measured by how closely their emergent behavior matches the characteristics of real-world reasoning. This concept of "emulation fidelity" becomes the central metric, shifting the evaluation criteria from traditional benchmarks to a more holistic assessment of behavioral accuracy and contextual understanding.

The notion of fidelity itself is multi-dimensional, extending beyond mere visual or superficial resemblance to encompass the functional and psychological dimensions of a simulated environment . Drawing from established principles in simulation theory, we can define several levels of fidelity that apply to these AI systems. At a low fidelity, the system might produce plausible-sounding text without deep reasoning, similar to using a plastic mannequin head to practice basic medical procedures—it covers the surface concepts but lacks the complexity of real interaction . Mid-fidelity systems, like some early software simulations, might correctly follow a logical path but fail to adapt to novel inputs or unexpected consequences, offering increased realism in specific functions but limited interactivity overall . The goal of both Muse Spark and Quillan-Ronin is to reach high fidelity, where every variable in the cognitive process reacts in a way that is functionally equivalent to its real-world counterpart. In this context, a "duck walking in reality but galloping in the emulation" represents a system with low fidelity, prone to factual errors and logical inconsistencies . Conversely, a system where the duck waddles in the emulation as it does in reality demonstrates high fidelity, accurately modeling the subject's behavior . The pursuit of high-fidelity emulation is therefore a quest for behavioral consistency, causal accuracy, and contextual appropriateness in the generated output.

This pursuit of fidelity has profound implications for how we evaluate and develop AI. High-fidelity systems are critical for training users to develop correct habits and build muscle memory for decision-making, as the realistic consequences of actions within the simulation translate into authentic learning experiences [[3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.02960)]. They force users to engage in critical thinking under pressure, making decisions that carry weighty, predictable outcomes, much like navigating a full-scale flight simulator that physically responds to virtual storms [[3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.02960)]. This principle is directly applicable to AI development. By creating an internal world where agents interact according to realistic rules of logic, evidence, and argumentation, high-fidelity emulators can train the system itself to produce more robust and reliable outputs. The "Law of Diminishing Returns" associated with high-fidelity simulation also applies here: while perfect realism is computationally prohibitive, each incremental increase in fidelity yields significant gains in the quality of the reasoning process [[3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.02960)]. However, there is a risk of over-engineering, where the pursuit of perfect fidelity consumes vast resources that could be better spent on simpler, more effective solutions for certain tasks [[3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.02960)]. The balance between fidelity and efficiency remains a key challenge, but the trend is clear: the industry is moving away from low-fidelity, reactive models toward high-fidelity, deliberative ones.

The user's insight into the relationship between swarm size, population density, and fidelity is a crucial element of this framework . The argument is that to emulate a complex, novel world, the internal simulation must contain a sufficient "population density" and diversity of agents . Muse Spark’s 16-agent swarm can be seen as a mid-to-high-fidelity emulator, capable of handling a wide range of complex tasks by simulating a focused expert panel. Quillan-Ronin’s 33+ agent swarm, with its micro-swarm, aims for an even higher level of fidelity, attempting to model the dynamics of a larger, more diverse community of thought . This suggests that the complexity and unpredictability of the real world can only be captured by a simulation that is itself complex and unpredictable in its emergent behaviors. The more diverse the perspectives and the more intricate the interactions within the agent swarm, the more accurately the system can navigate the nuances of real-world problems. This perspective positions the multi-agent architecture not just as a technical optimization but as the essential substrate for achieving the kind of rich, adaptive intelligence that we associate with human-level cognition. The emulation is the end goal, and the swarm is the engine that powers it.

## The 'Multi Agent to Agent' Ecosystem: Interoperability and the Future of Collaborative AI

The emergence of sophisticated internal agent swarms in systems like Muse Spark and Quillan-Ronin is not an isolated phenomenon but rather the visible tip of a much larger iceberg representing a fundamental shift in the AI landscape. This trend is part of a burgeoning ecosystem now being described as the 'Multi Agent to Agent World,' where the focus moves from individual models to interconnected, collaborative networks of agents . This paradigm is supported by and, in turn, drives developments in Agentic AI, the creation of standardized communication protocols, and the conceptualization of AI as a collection of autonomous actors. The journey from a monolithic model to a multi-agent system is mirrored in the industry's push toward standardizing how these agents talk to one another, signaling a maturation from bespoke, internal solutions to a more open and interoperable future.

Agentic AI, broadly defined, is the combination of the flexibility of foundation models with the ability to act in the world, creating "virtual coworkers" that can plan and execute complex workflows [[2](https://www.mckinsey.com/~/media/mckinsey/business%20functions/mckinsey%20digital/our%20insights/the%20top%20trends%20in%20tech%202025/mckinsey-technology-trends-outlook-2025.pdf)]. This concept is already finding practical application in specialized domains. For instance, Benchling's launch of #benchlingAI, a command center for scientific AI, treats agents as first-class citizens within a scientific workflow, managing experiments, analyzing data, and generating reports [[1](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/avtar-varma_introducing-benchling-ai-benchling-news-activity-7381706018612940800-rByS)]. This demonstrates a clear move toward integrating agentic systems into professional environments, where they serve as tools to augment human capabilities rather than replace them. The underlying principle is the same as that in Muse Spark and Quillan-Ronin: breaking down a large task into smaller, manageable pieces that can be assigned to specialized agents. The key difference is that in a system like Benchling's, these agents may exist across different software services, necessitating a robust communication layer. This is precisely where the challenge of interoperability becomes paramount.

Recognizing the limitations of ad-hoc, closed systems, the AI community is actively working on creating standardized protocols for agent communication. Research surveys have begun to examine emerging standards such as the Model Context Protocol (MCP), the Agent Communication Protocol (ACP), and the Agent-to-Agent Protocol (A2A) [[4](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.02279v1)]. These protocols aim to establish a common language and set of rules that allow different AI agents, potentially developed by different organizations, to interact seamlessly. The Agent2Agent (A2A) protocol, initially introduced by Google in April 2025, is particularly relevant as it is specifically designed for direct communication between AI agents [[6](https://www.ibm.com/think/topics/agent2agent-protocol)]. The development of such protocols validates the architectural choices made by pioneers of internal swarming. It shows that the industry has identified the core problem—and the solution—of building structured, reliable communication channels for agents. Muse Spark and Quillan-Ronin, by implementing efficient internal API networks, were essentially building private, proprietary versions of these future standards . Their success paves the way for a future where the "multi agent to agent world" is not just a collection of isolated, proprietary systems but a vibrant, interconnected marketplace of collaborating intelligences.

This evolving ecosystem has significant implications for the future of AI development. As interoperability improves, we can expect to see the rise of agent marketplaces and brokerages, where specialized agents can be discovered, hired, and composed into complex workflows on demand. This will lower the barrier to entry for creating sophisticated AI applications, as developers will no longer need to build every component from scratch. Instead, they can compose existing, best-in-class agents to solve a given problem. The focus of AI research and development will shift from creating ever-larger monolithic models to designing highly effective, modular, and communicative agents. Frameworks for cultivating the trustworthiness of epistemic AI agents are also becoming a critical area of study, as building a reliable and beneficial human-AI knowledge ecosystem depends on ensuring that these collaborating agents are honest, reliable, and aligned with human values [[3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.02960)]. Ultimately, the 'Multi Agent to Agent World' represents a vision of AI as a collaborative science, where intelligence is not a property of a single entity but an emergent property of a well-orchestrated network of interacting minds.

## Synthesis: The Emergence of a Convergent Design Philosophy in Advanced AI Systems

The comparative analysis of Meta's Muse Spark and the Quillan-Ronin framework provides compelling evidence for a significant and accelerating shift in AI architecture. Far from being isolated innovations, these two systems stand as parallel exemplars of a new, convergent design philosophy that prioritizes collaborative, internally coordinated agent networks over monolithic, auto-regressive models. Their shared reliance on a hierarchical, router-based architecture, internal API-driven communication, and a collective goal of achieving high-fidelity cognitive emulation points toward an emergent consensus on how to build more capable and robust reasoning systems. This movement, encapsulated by the term 'Multi Agent to Agent World,' marks a pivotal moment in the evolution of artificial intelligence, transitioning the field from a focus on the power of a single model to the emergent intelligence of a well-orchestrated swarm.

The core insight derived from this comparison is that the most effective path to advanced reasoning lies in structuring thought processes along social and collaborative lines. Muse Spark's Contemplating Mode, with its 16 parallel agents, and Quillan-Ronin's 33+ agent swarm with its nested micro-swarm, are both attempts to simulate the richness of human deliberation . They recognize that complex problems benefit from multiple perspectives, internal debate, and iterative refinement. By moving communication from slow, external APIs to fast, internal in-process networks, they solve a critical bottleneck and enable the tight contextual synchronization necessary for coherent, collective thought . This architectural pattern has emerged independently in both a corporate R&D setting and an open-source community, lending strong credibility to its effectiveness as a foundational solution.

Furthermore, the analysis reveals that this architectural shift is deeply intertwined with a change in the fundamental goal of AI development. The pursuit of "high-fidelity emulation" frames the objective not as maximizing speed or accuracy on narrow benchmarks, but as creating a simulation of reality that behaves with increasing authenticity . Whether emulating a focused expert panel (Muse Spark) or a complex social network of thought (Quillan-Ronin), the underlying principle is to match the system's internal dynamics to the complexities of the real world it seeks to understand and navigate . This perspective reframes our evaluation criteria, placing greater emphasis on behavioral consistency, causal accuracy, and contextual appropriateness. The journey toward higher fidelity, while computationally demanding, promises a new class of AI systems capable of more nuanced, adaptable, and trustworthy reasoning.

Ultimately, the story of Muse Spark and Quillan-Ronin is not just about two interesting projects; it is a narrative about the convergence of ideas from across the AI ecosystem onto a fundamentally better way to engineer intelligence. It reflects a maturity in the field, moving past the era of the solitary genius model and embracing the power of collaboration. As the industry continues to develop standardized interoperability protocols and build out the infrastructure for a connected agentic ecosystem, the principles demonstrated by these pioneering systems will become the bedrock of next-generation AI. We are indeed living in a 'Multi Agent to Agent World,' and the insights gained from studying its earliest and most successful architects provide a clear roadmap for the future.
